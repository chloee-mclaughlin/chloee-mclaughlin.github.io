Generation & applications of pangenomic datasets

A reference genome provides an important foundation for genomic research, but a single genome cannot capture the full extent of genetic diversity within a species. Structural variation, gene presence–absence variation, and other forms of sequence diversity can be missed or underrepresented when genomic analyses rely on a single reference. Pangenomic resources address this limitation by incorporating genomic information from multiple individuals, providing a more comprehensive representation of species-wide diversity.
I apply pangenomic resources for improved characterization and analysis of genetic diversity. By integrating diverse genome assemblies, sequence variation, and population-scale genomic datasets, we better capture complex genomic variation and enable more comprehensive investigations of evolutionary history, local adaptation, and agronomically important traits.

Linking (a)biotic gradients to adaptive variation
Understanding how environmental gradients shape genetic and phenotypic diversity is central to identifying the mechanisms underlying local adaptation. Both abiotic factors (including temperature, precipitation, and soil properties) and biotic interactions, such as host–parasite relationships, can impose selective pressures on natural and agricultural populations.
Genotype-environment associations (GEAs) describe a suite of methods which identify loci associated with environmental variation, but determining which environmental factors, or combinations of factors, underlie patterns of selection remains a challenge. Integrating multivariate environmental gradients with phenotypic variation offers an opportunity to better characterize these relationships and identify putatively adaptive genetic variation. To this end, we developed an approach that predicts trait variation across multivariate environmental gradients and projects these predictions onto larger collections of georeferenced genotypes for genome-wide association (GWA) analyses. This approach has been applied to investigate adaptive variation in landrace maize, including root anatomy, microbiomes, and seminal root number.
In my graduate studies, I investigated how host–parasite co-evolution shapes host adaptive strategies and associated physiological trade-offs. Striga hermonthica, a root-parasitic plant, is a major constraint to cereal production in Sub-Saharan Africa. Different sorghum ecotypes exhibit distinct resistance strategies, hypothesized to have evolved in response to local parasite prevalence. By integrating phenotypic and physiological approaches, I investigated how these resistance strategies relate to host–parasite interactions and characterize associated trade-offs. Paper:Strigolactone effects on Sorghum bicolor ecophysiology and symbioses.

Projecting maladaptation & identifying optimal genotypes under novel climates

Changing climates threaten plant populations (native and agricultural) adapted to historical local environmental conditions. At the same time, the intraspecific genetic diversity of a species may provide opportunities to reduce maladaptation (genetic mismatch between existing populations and projected environmental conditions) through the identification and deployment of better-adapted genotypes. I use methods which combine GEA approaches and climate forcasting models to predict patterns of comtemporary putatively adaptive diversity, project genetic change required to track a change in climate, and determine genotypes which are maintain or ameliorate predicted disruptions to adaptation under novel climates.

  • Investigating how species-wide genetic diversity and patterns of local adaptation can inform the identification of genotypes suited to future environmental conditions, with applications for crop improvement and management. Paper: Leveraging species-wide variation and patterns of adaptation to inform pecan crop improvement efforts
  • Examining how abrupt and extreme climate disruptions affect native crop genotype-environment relationships, quantifing the extent and geographic distribution of maladaptation, and identifying potentially suitable varietal substitutions. We find that these catastrophic events could result in widespread global maladaptation of crop landraces, with suitable varietal substitutions potentially unavailable for many locations. Paper: Maladaptation in cereal crop landraces following a soot-producing climate catastrophe
