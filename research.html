Generation & applications of pangenomic datasets

A reference genome provides an important foundation for genomic research, but a single genome cannot capture the full extent of genetic diversity within a species. Structural variation, gene presence–absence variation, and other forms of sequence diversity can be missed or underrepresented when genomic analyses rely on a single reference. Pangenomic resources address this limitation by incorporating genomic information from multiple individuals, providing a more comprehensive representation of species-wide diversity. I am interested in applying pangenomic resources to improve how we characterize, analyze, and make use of genetic diversity. By integrating diverse genome assemblies, sequence variation, and population-scale genomic datasets, we can develop tools that better capture complex genomic variation and enable more comprehensive investigations of evolutionary history, local adaptation, and agronomically important traits.

Linking (a)biotic gradients to adaptive variation
Understanding how environmental variation shapes genetic and phenotypic diversity is central to identifying the mechanisms underlying local adaptation. Both abiotic factors (such as temperature, precipitation, soil properties), and biotic interactions, such as host–parasite relationships, can impose selection on natural and agricultural populations. Genotype-environment associations (GEAs) are useful for identifying environmentally associated loci, but it is often unclear which environmental factors or combinations of factors drive observed patterns of selection. I have developed a method that predicts trait variation across multivariate environmental gradients. Fitted models are then used to project trait values onto larger collections of georeferenced genotypes, enabling genome-wide association (GWA) analyses of environmentally associated adaptive variation. I have applied this approach to investigate adaptive variation in landrace maize, including: Root anatomy | Microbiomes | Seminal root number I also investigate how host–parasite co-evolution shapes adaptive strategies and associated physiological trade-offs. Striga hermonthica, a root-parasitic plant, is a major constraint to cereal production in Sub-Saharan Africa. Different sorghum ecotypes exhibit distinct resistance strategies, hypothesized to have evolved in response to local parasite prevalence. By integrating phenotypic and physiological approaches, I investigate how these resistance strategies relate to host–parasite interactions and characterize the associated trade-offs.

Projecting maladaptation & identifying optimal genotypes under novel climates

Changing climates threaten native and agricultural plant populations whose genotypes have adapted to historical local environmental conditions. However, the breadth of genetic diversity within a species may provide opportunities to mitigate maladaptation (genetic mismatch between existing populations and projected environmental conditions) through the identification and deployment of better-adapted genotypes.
I am interested in extending contemporary genotype-environment association (GEA) approaches beyond identifying loci associated with current environmental conditions toward predicting maladaptation and identifying potentially optimal genotypes under novel climates. By characterizing existing genotype-environment relationships and projecting them onto future environmental conditions, these approaches can quantify the geographic extent of maladaptation and identify opportunities for adaptation through the movement or substitution of genotypes.

Leveraging species-wide variation and patterns of adaptation to inform pecan crop improvement efforts
In pecan, I investigate how species-wide genetic diversity and patterns of local adaptation can inform the identification of genotypes suited to future environmental conditions, with applications for crop improvement and management.
Maladaptation in cereal crop landraces following a soot-producing climate catastrophe
I have also applied this framework to examine how abrupt and extreme climate disruptions affect crop genotype-environment relationships. Using soot-induced climate change, such as that associated with nuclear winter, as a case study, we quantify the extent and geographic distribution of maladaptation and identify potentially suitable varietal substitutions. We find that these catastrophic events could result in widespread global maladaptation of crop landraces, with suitable varietal substitutions potentially unavailable for many locations.
Together, these applications demonstrate how extending contemporary GEA approaches can help anticipate the consequences of environmental change and identify opportunities to maintain or improve adaptation under novel climates.
